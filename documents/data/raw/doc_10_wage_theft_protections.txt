The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), administered and enforced by the Wage and Hour Division of the United States Department of Labor (DOL), establishes comprehensive federal mandates governing minimum wage thresholds, mandatory overtime compensation, and record-keeping standards across the domestic workforce. A critical and frequently violated subsection of the FLSA explicitly regulates the legality of paycheck deductions executed by employers. Many business operators attempt to insulate their profit margins by forcing workers to pay for operational overhead, inventory mistakes, or business losses through automated paycheck subtractions.

Under the strict regulatory fact sheets issued by the DOL, an employer is legally barred from deducting money from an employee's wages to cover cash register shortages, broken tools, damaged merchandise, or customer walk-outs ("dine and dash" events) if that specific deduction causes the employee's net hourly wage rate to fall below the mandated federal minimum wage floor for that specific workweek. For example, if a worker is paid exactly the minimum wage, any employer-enforced deduction for a broken kitchen tool or cash drawer discrepancy is an immediate federal violation of the FLSA, constituting explicit wage theft.

Even if an employee earns an hourly wage significantly higher than the federal minimum floor, specific deductions can still trigger violations if they infringe upon mandatory overtime calculations. Under the FLSA, paycheck deductions for business operational losses can never cut into an employee's mandatory time-and-a-half overtime pay for hours worked beyond 40 hours in a single workweek. Any agreement where an employee signs a contract "voluntarily authorizing" an employer to make deductions that drop their net compensation below the legal minimum wage floor is completely null and void under federal law; statutory consumer and worker rights cannot be bargained away through private employment waivers.
