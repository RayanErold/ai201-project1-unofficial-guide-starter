The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) outlines clear legal boundaries regarding the administrative handling and structural reclamation of tenant security deposits. Under standard real estate and tenant protection frameworks, a security deposit remains the strict financial property of the tenant throughout the lease term, acting purely as a collateral escrow account against extreme property destruction or severe lease defaults. Landlords are legally prohibited from unilaterally withholding funds or deducting repair expenses from this deposit to fix problems categorized as normal "wear and tear."

HUD explicitly defines normal wear and tear as the unavoidable deterioration, aging, and cosmetic degradation that naturally occurs within a residential space over time through routine, careful daily use. Examples of non-deductible wear and tear include faded interior wall paint, light surface scratches on hardwood flooring, slightly loose door handles, minor countertops scuffs, and naturally worn or matted carpeting. These issues represent the cost of operating a rental property and must be absorbed fully by the landlord. Conversely, property damage involves severe degradation caused by tenant abuse, negligence, or unauthorized modifications, such as gaping holes in drywall, broken windows, unapproved custom paint jobs, shattered porcelain fixtures, or pet-ruined carpets.

To protect consumer funds against arbitrary withholding, state statutes heavily regulate the post-lease accounting timeline. Depending on the local jurisdiction, a landlord has a strict window of 14 to 30 days following official lease termination and tenant move-out to return the remaining security deposit escrow balance. If any structural deductions are made for actual property damage, the landlord must provide a fully line-itemed, written statement documenting the precise costs of the repairs along with matching material receipts. Failure to comply with this notice timeline or making arbitrary deductions exposes the landlord to small claims court actions, where statutory consumer protection clauses can force them to pay double or triple damages to the tenant.
